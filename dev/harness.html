Show and grade — the board ring (section 23)

In the real thing this board wears Chessable's own board image, copied off the live page, so it follows whichever theme the user has picked. The checkerboard below stands in for it. The ring is 4px amber plus a 1px dark edge: at 2px it sat on a busy green border and read as part of the board rather than as a frame. Thicker rather than a new colour, because the colour carries meaning — amber is "this board takes no input", against the green of the drill and the line quiz, which do.

Left to right: your card — the arrow is up, and it means the board has stopped and is waiting for you to grade, not "this is the move" (the piece and the two tinted squares already said that). Then the same card auto-played: no arrow, because it grades itself a beat later and there is no decision to point at. Then your card over a light theme. Then the same card in blindfold (section 23b): the pieces are gone and everything else stays — the board itself is what covers Chessable's, and the tint and the arrow say which move was played, which the mode announces in words anyway. Then the quiz board for comparison — green ring, our own squares, and it takes moves.

The arrow is green — the stylesheet's own, drawn with no colour argument at all. An amber one (matching the ring, the theory being that one colour should mean "waiting for you") was tried in v0.77.0–0.80.0 and blends into Chessable's own green board image; the ring keeps the amber because it is not on the board image. This replica said amber until v0.88.0, which was simply stale.

Show and grade — the settings popover (section 23)

Behind the cog, because these are persistent preferences and everything else in this row acts on the session in front of you (DESIGN-ui-inventory §F4). Body-mounted and fixed in the real thing — inside the row it would change the widget's height, which the Lichess panel, the alt-check chip and the heads-up all anchor below. Pace is the beat before each move is shown; animation is how long a piece takes to slide, and the advance beat is floored at it so the two cannot be set against each other into one blurred move.

The last three are the AUDIO (section 23b, v0.88.0). Three rows and not one, because they are independent: reading the moves out with the pieces up is the ordinary way to use it, and Blindfold works with the audio off too, since the moves are printed in the panel. Blindfold takes the PIECES off our board rather than the board — our board is what covers Chessable's, so not drawing it would leave their position on screen, which is blindfold practice with the answer in front of you.

Course tree — the column browser (section 24)

Course pages, beside the coverage panel. Miller columns over the course's own position graph: the board follows the selection, every row is a folded forced run, and the weight bar is how many of the course's lines take that move. Numbers below are the real ones from DESIGN-course-tree.md §12 (course 291847).

The real panel fills the window (inset: 16px) and measures how many columns fit; the cards here are sized replicas, so read the ROW and COLUMN metrics from them rather than the panel's own. A column holding a single move is never drawn — it is not a decision, so the walk takes it automatically and the breadcrumb carries it. Variation names appear only on the SELECTED row.

The Lichess scan (24b) is the opt-in toggle in the header. It adds a share chip to every row, a covers N% chip to every column head, and a block of the moves real games play that the course does NOT — amber where it is the OPPONENT to move (a reply you will meet and have no answer to), neutral where it is your own move (a repertoire decision, not a hole). Gap rows are deliberately NOT navigable: there is no subtree behind them.

Key moves & difficult moves (section 25)

Course pages, third in the bottom-right stack. The report answers one question: which moves the user actually gets wrong are sitting OUTSIDE the key range Chessable calculated, i.e. played for them and never asked for under Key Moves Only. Numbers below are the real ones from a live run on course 453453 (DESIGN-key-moves.md §6).

Colour, per DESIGN-ui-inventory §3. The launcher is a NEUTRAL outline, like the course tree's — the coverage panel keeps the corner's one filled green. A difficult move OUTSIDE the range is AMBER ("you have N outstanding items"); one inside is the plain neutral chip, because it is a fact about the course rather than a thing to do. The write is green outline while it offers and FILLED once armed — the second press is the one that changes the course.

A DASHED chip has no record in this variation (25c). Chessable keeps a separate history per card, so the same move in the same position can be difficult in one line and unrecorded in twenty others — and a move in a run-up is never asked there, so it can never be recorded at all. Those chips carry a figure POOLED from elsewhere in the course, and say so in their tooltip; a percentage on a card that has no record would otherwise read as a measurement of it. The amber line above the options is the count before arming: this toggle is the one setting here that can take a press from five variations to seventy.

Restoring is a second, separate control (25b). Lowering only ever adds to what is quizzed and restoring only ever takes away, so one armed press must never mean both: two buttons, two arming states. A restorable row is marked "can go back" in dim text with a rule down its left edge — deliberately NOT amber, which here means "you have an outstanding item", and a range that can go back is the opposite: a piece of work that is finished.

Intuition storm (section 27)

Launched from the COURSE TREE's header, not the corner — that corner already holds four things. A full-window panel of its own (inset: 16px, section 24's shape) with two EMPTY slots: section 18's board and its panel are pinned onto them by positionInHost, exactly as 24g pins the analysis board into the tree. Nothing is drawn into them, so they are outlined and labelled here the way 24g's are.

The verdict is win probability lost (§16), not centipawns: userHiCp admits positions up to +200, and giving up 100cp from +500 leaves you winning where giving up 100cp from 0 does not. FIVE bands rather than three, because the old middle was a cliff — 71cp at equality scored the same −2 as hanging a queen. At an equal position the two scoring bands land where the old flat 30cp/70cp ones did (33cp and 65cp), so this is a change of MEANING rather than of difficulty.

Colour means one thing. Green is the streak multiplier and nothing else — it is the reward being counted. The clock goes AMBER when it runs low ("you have N seconds outstanding", the row palette's own meaning), never red: red already means three things in this file. The verdict line's numbers wear .clo-cdb--est, because every one of them is a ChessDB learn=1 figure and presenting one as a book eval is the thing this feature must never do.

The clock is tabular-nums and right-aligned with a min-width: a proportional "1" is narrower than a "0", so a counting-down clock jitters every tick and the eye reads that as the panel moving.

The ply range (§14.9) — and the review (§14.10)

The range shows only when NO clock is running — before the first card and after the last — because changing it mid-session would describe an exercise the positions already queued are not from. Those are also the two moments the user is waiting rather than playing, which is why it needs no surface of its own.

The review is up instead of the stage: same two halves, same widths, so nothing appears to move when the session ends — only what is in each half. The verdict colour is a LEFT EDGE, never a fill: twenty filled rows are twenty coloured blocks with the moves unreadable on them, and the point of the list is to find the misses in one column without reading it.

The performance record (§15)

A FOURTH thing the body can show, up instead of the stage, the review and the summary — and reachable only when no clock is running, for a harder reason than the ply selectors': section 18's board is pinned to the stage's slots in DOCUMENT coordinates, so hiding the stage under a live card would leave a board floating over the table.

The strip is §16's five bands as one ramp — green, dim green, neutral, amber, red — which is green for the two that SCORE, nothing for the one worth nothing, and the row palette's own "outstanding" and "danger" for the two that COST. The same five tokens colour the review's row edges, defined once in the stylesheet beside them.

Two tables, two questions, both of which were asked for: which REPERTOIRE do I read worst, and which CHAPTER of the one in front of me. Rows are sorted WORST FIRST — a table whose top row is your best line answers nothing — the course being stormed is MARKED rather than moved (pulling it out of that order would break the only thing the sort is for), and every row carries its answer count, because a 0% built on one answer is not the claim a 0% built on twenty is.

The strip is over the GRADED answers only. unknown is a fact about ChessDB, not about the user — finishAnswer scores it nothing and holds the streak for exactly that reason — so it is out of both halves of the fraction and out of the bar; it is named in the bar's tooltip instead. found is the two bands that SCORE: an inaccuracy is not a move found, it is "no, but it did not matter much", which is what avg lost beside it is for.

Under 700px of PANEL width the STRIP is what goes, not a number: the percentage beside it says the same thing less precisely, and the two numbers are what the table is read for.
